Hanna Serykava — hserykava@ucsd.edu

Proposition: The number of abortion clinics in a state is a key driver of abortion rates.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Dropping states with 0% change in clinics: +1.5; Dropping states with 0% change from the graph was part of essential design of the visualization, because my visualization specifically focuses on how change in abortion clinics affects the abortion rate in different states. This was done to properly explore the proposition “The number of abortion clinics in a state is a key driver of abortion rates” so I only isolated states with a change in number of abortion clinics to visualize the results of these changes. Keeping 0% change states in the graph made the pattern significantly less linear, making the visualization less persuasive and the trend less apparent, so choice was made to remove it from the final visualization. This removal could have deleted some useful information from the graph, but this sacrifice was made to keep clarity and focus of the visualization.
  • Using rolling average for abortion rates: +1; Rolling average was used to emphasize the trend of the data more clearly and reduce noise, creating a more obvious, more linear pattern for the viewers. Without the rolling average the line of change in abortion rate was very jumpy, so it was very hard to distinguish the trend. Alternatively, I might have chosen to not use the rolling average and make the viewers try to distinguish the pattern themselves, but I believe this option would drastically decrease the strength of my visualization. However, using the rolling average is susceptible to the risk of suggesting continuity or creating a casual trend in the data where this trend may not exist.
  • Usage of a persuasive title: -1; The title "More Clinics, More abortions: A Clear Link Across U.S. States" may appear misleading to some viewers if they interpret it as causation instead of correlation, but this title was created to highlight positive correlation visible on the visualization. I considered labelling it according to x and y-axis, but the purpose of the visualization became less apparent, and it made it more complicated for viewers to understand the proposition explored by the graph. I decided to use more engaging title for graph, but made my visualization susceptible to misleading if viewers interpret title stating causation.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Filtering out extreme outlier: -0.5; District of Columbia was removed from the visualization because its change in abortion rate was significantly larger than that of other states, which compressed the scale of bins on the graph and made it harder to distinguish the patterns on the rest of the data. Deleting district of Columbia made trend on the graph more visible. Alternatively, it could have been included (could also have been marked as an outlier), but that would have made the visualization less effective and didn’t provide any valuable information in return because this dramatic decline in abortion greatly differs from all other states. Not including this outlier, however, could risk adding bias to my visualization.
  • Truncating the y-axis: -1; I lowered bound of the y-axis and set it to -40% to better highlight variation in abortion rates above and below zero. Truncating the y-axis may visually made positive and negative sides more similar, but it was done for viewers to focus on both sides of change in abortion rates, negative and positive. I believe not truncating the y-axis made the graph appear more negative, completely overshadowing the positive change in abortion rate when both have quite significant impact against the proposition. Keeping visualization without truncating the y-axis would add more data for the graph, but I believe it doesn’t add value to the overall visualization, which strives to illustrate the difference in change in abortion rates. However, this may make viewers assume equality between two sides, which is deceptive.
  • Usage of persuasive title: -1; Title and subtitle use language which could be considered quite strong, but the graph includes states with abortion rate changes exceeding -30% and +30%, therefore I used the wording "wildly" and "dramatically" to highlight and emphasize the pattern of the visualization. A more neutral title that described only the graph’s axes was possible, but it made the purpose and focus of the chart less clear to viewers. This title may introduce my visualization to the risk of the exaggeration of the graph results for the viewers which is an ethical risk.

Final Reflection

Working with the task of creating opposite claims was very challenging, especially working with the abortions data set with quite a few ethical problems it could cause. Showing correlation between number of abortions and number of clinics felt quite straightforward because their relationship resembled a relationship between supply and demand. What was complicated, however, was creating visualization proving the opposite claim, that the number of abortion clinics in a state is not a key driver of abortion rates, and it took me many attempts visualizing the data before the results showed a satisfactory counter to my first visualization. Visualizing the graph for proposition proved to be less complicated and more straightforward. I worked with scatterplot to find some patterns in the data and when I found them, I created a visualization for them. However, second visualization, proved to be quite difficult. I used multiple different transformations of the data before I found a pattern I could work with. Finding the data which shows information for and against the proposition helped me use a smaller number of deceptive tools. Surprisingly, the hardest ethical part was creating titles and subtitles for my visualizations, because there is a very thin line between honest and deceptive titles which is very hard to distinguish. I had to constantly ask myself the question: “does using this title mislead or honestly help the viewers of my visualization?” I believe there is no 100% definition of the “ethical analysis and visualization”, because any data transformation produces ethical risks, even though very slight sometimes. I believe the ethical analysis of the visualization lies in the hands of the person working with the data, if the person tries to create a pattern which isn’t observed in the data by performing too many transformations, or deleting too many outliers, resulting in this person is creating something that isn’t there, creates a breach of ethics. If the person working with the data has personal beliefs they try to prove using the data, ignoring what the data actually illustrates, this person makes unethical visualization. I believe it is impossible to draw a line between “acceptable” and “misleading” persuasive choices, because data transformations which are ethical for one specific dataset are unethical for the other, therefore, these choices are in the hands of the person working with transforming the data and this line will only become more distinguishable with experience of this specific person.
